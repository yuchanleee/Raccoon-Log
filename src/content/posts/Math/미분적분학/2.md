### L'Hôpital's Rule

Let $a,L\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty,\infty\}$ be extended real numbers, and let $I$ be an open interval containing $a\,(\text{if }a\in\mathbb{R})$ or having $a$ as an endpoint$\,(\text{if }a=\pm\infty)$

Let $f,g:I\setminus\{a\}\to\mathbb{R}$ be differential functions on $I\setminus\{a\}$ such that 
$g'(x)\neq 0 \quad \text{for all} \quad x\in I\setminus\{a\}$

Suppose that one of the following conditions holds:
1. $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \lim_{x \to a} g(x) = 0$
2. $\lim_{x \to a} \lvert g(x) \rvert= \infty$

If the limit of the raio of their derivatives exists, that is,
$$
\lim_{x \to a}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}=L
$$
Then the limit of ratio of the functions exists and is given by: 
$$
\lim_{x \to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=L
$$

<br>

#### Proof of the $0/0$ case 

First suppose that $a,L\in\mathbb{R}$

we may define $f(a)=g(a)=0$ with these definitions, f and g are continuous at a

By definitions of a limit, 
$$
\forall \varepsilon>0,\ \exists \delta>0
\quad \text{s.t.} \quad
0<\lvert y-a\rvert<\delta
\quad \Longrightarrow \quad
\left|\frac{f'(y)}{g'(y)}-L\right|<\varepsilon
$$

Take $x$ such that

$$
0<\lvert x-a\rvert<\delta.
$$

By the **Cauchy Mean Value Theorem**, there exists $c$ strictly between $a$ and $x$ such that

$$
\frac{f(x)-f(a)}{g(x)-g(a)}
=
\frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)}.
$$

Since $f(a)=g(a)=0$,

$$
\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}
=
\frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)}.
$$

Since $c$ lies between $a$ and $x$,

$$
0<\lvert c-a\rvert<\lvert x-a\rvert<\delta.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\left|\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}-L\right|
=
\left|\frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)}-L\right|
<\varepsilon.
$$

Hence,

$$
\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=L.
$$
